The stars are beautiful, aren't they? Reminds me of sporadic development. Scattered buildings built haphazardly along roads with no grand plan behind them. People deciding to build houses wherever they please, wasting resources and creating inefficiency out of sheer convenience. Usually, the thought would bother me, but looking up at the stars, seeing how they're placed along the sky with no discernable pattern, I feel at peace. Some things just don't need to be planned. Marisa's dead body is right here. She's in algor mortis. I only have a little bit of time before it reaches rigor mortis. It wouldn't change anything, but that's when it will really hit me. I take a look at the entrenching tool resting in my survey kit. Funny. The only time any of it has actually come in handy. I grab the tool with my free hand. The other's still gripping tightly to my prismatic compass. Getting on my knees, I begin excavating the spoil. I'm not thinking of anything in particular. It's just mechanical. The soil is well graded. It's ironic. The clearing here, right next to the downstream river, secluded and quiet. It's the absolute perfect spot for a hydro-electric station. I'll never tell the COR or the Cardónians, of course. The hole looks big enough. I grab her body. It's not warm anymore. That's the algor mortis. With moderate effort, but major stress on my legs, I drop her into the hole. Getting on my knees again, I push the spoil over her with my tool. I take one last look at her face before covering it up. She still looks beautiful. I grab her copy of the Dictation from the ship. I don't know how to use this thing. I don't usually deal with poetry. Flipping through the pages, I think that there has to be some kind of funeral rite in here. My eyes glance over the page marked Requiescats. That's gotta be it. I flip through it until I found one I like, then clear my throat and begin to read aloud. > And it was so that like the Architect above, there is one of the deceased who his children loved. And like the Architect above we do not forget, we honor their life so their creation may beget. I also take my flask out of my coat pocket and pour it on the made ground. I don't know why I do it, but I do. Then I take out my field book. > 15 vi 27. Ontembaar, N. 118° to point of rocks. Ground unsuitable. I get back in the boat and leave. Following the stars for navigation. Despite their random placement, or maybe because of it, they still have use.
It all starts in Krios of '27. That's when the COR sends me to Ontembaar. I get word from my superior Enguerrand Badeaux. He walks into my office with the stride of a man who feels he could reshape the world with his word alone. "Dabinett, have you been drinking?" he asks, the disgust in his tone palpable. I have. I feel no reason to lie to him. "Yes." Enguerrand's face doesn't change. "I know. You're a slave to your impulses." This is his way of trying to reshape my behavior. He always thinks appealing to the tradition of the Tanner thinker Glædwine is the best way to get through to me. It isn't, and I can clearly see through it. "There's beauty in being a slave. You're fed, you have shelter. What's a little manual labor?" Clearly wanting to change the subject, Enguerrand proceeds. "They want you to go to Ontembaar. The Council is making a proclamation soon about the mandate." "Why do they want me?" I smile. I just like to hear him say it. Enguerrand's eyelid twitches. "They want you on the ground to plan the Cardónian city." "What about Henri? Surely the Cardónians want a city with some le montagnard influence?" Enguerrand is livid. "They want you." "Why?" Enguerrand is already at the door. "You're to be at the docks at 0500. Don't oversleep." I take a look at the map on my wall. Ontembaar is three weeks by ship from Linolia. I don't like ships. They're so unplanned. A hundred and ten people on average, all trapped together in a metal rookery. Nevertheless, they want me, like Enguerrand said. I could never resist the siren call of having tens of thousands all using my plans. A beautifully calibrated machine that could not exist without me. I begin packing my tools.
I'm seasick. The noxious waft of brine isn't helping. Staring at the churning plane of water off the verandah, I'm getting the dizzying feeling that it stretches endlessly. The sea and sky just dissolve into an undifferentiated gray plane as far as I can look. I need to distract myself. Leaving the balcony, I open my bag and take out my grid paper, drafting triangle, and parallel rule. This is my element. I lay down some marks on the grid without thinking. Ordo ab chao, as it's called in the architecturale school. You make random marks, then try to connect them in a functional manner. They say this is how the Architect designed the world, creating order out of chaos, but I don't buy that. It's simply apophenia and horror vacui that make this work. Our brains abhor a blank space, and connecting these marks is our hardwired manner of finding meaning in sensory noise. Connecting the points, I immediately see the perfect spot for a water channel. From that, naturally comes the sluice gate and hydroelectric station. Now I see it. Twelve chains from that, at this other mark, the only logical construction is the substation. I mark the high tension feeder line with ink. From there, the math dictates the rest. The copper mains will branch north along the towpath, while the secondary lines run west into the planned tenements. The— KNOCK KNOCK. Someone's at the door. "I'm busy!" I yell, aggravated at having my train of thought derailed. Twelve chains gives the transformers enough clearance from the damp spray of the tailrace, yet— KNOCK KNOCK KNOCK. I slam my hand on the table and angrily get up. Swinging the door open, I spit, "What is it?!" "Mr. Dabinett, is that you?" I didn't even look at who it was at the door. In front of me, I see an Amaranthian boy, maybe 12 or 13. I'm assuming he works down at the coal bunkers, judging by the thick layer of soot on his clothes. "Who's asking?" "My name is Naeem. I'm a huge fan of your work. I kind of want to be a planner when I grow up." "Yeah, yeah. I'm busy, kid." "Oh. I just wanted to ask if you would sign—" "Look, ammie, I'm in the middle of something important. Why don't you head back to the bunkers? And send for my turbot. I haven't gotten it yet." "…Yes, sir, Mr. Dabinett." He walks away, his head down. Good. The transmission loss will be negligible if the secondary lines run into the main tenements. It isn't divine inspiration. It's simple math and geometry. You keep the secondary constructions away from the prime construction, and it will always work beautifully.
The Ischemic War really tore apart Ontembaar. I've heard about the reports, but stepping off the boat and seeing it firsthand, it's no wonder they sent me. The hydro infrastructure was clearly overdrafted, no doubt due to the war effort. I glance in horror at the bleached, crusted waterline, seeing how the timber pilings give way to rotting riprap and stranded flatboats. Part of the dock is in rubble, which I take minor effort to step over. Naeem holds my bag and walks in tandem. "I hope you can fix this, Mr. Dabinett," he squeaks in his grating prepubescent voice. "Of course I can. That's why the COR pays me the big Stands." "Stands?" he questions. I glance at him. "I forgot you haven't adopted them yet. Standards. The global reserve currency introduced by us. My 'superior' Badeaux actually ran a few designs by me." He looks confused. "Global reserve currency?" I grimace as I see his dirty fingernails clasp the bag handle. "Forget it. Just be careful with that bag. It's worth more than this whole country." The confusion subsides. "Before you leave, can you please sign this book, Mr. Dabinett? It would mean a lot to me." He hands me a ragged tome. The Beginner's Introduction to Le Montagnard Design, by Henri Legrand. "Get that book out of my face." I spit on the ground. "Pff, Le Montagnard design. Let me give you a tip, kid. Quit while you're behind. Leave urban planning to the Linolians." I take a swig from my flask. "What do you drink?" Naeem asks me. "Why do you care? It's absinthe." "Absinthe? Okay." I see him writing it down on the front page of the book. Finally, we arrive at the customs office. I reach into my pocket and produce my passport. The guard looks up in shock. "Claude Dabinett? You're Dabinett? Wow. You look more… rugged… than I expected." My tie is loose and my hair's a mess. I never really cared. "Can I go?" "Of course, sir. We have a lorry prepared for you." I take my bag and I'm about to walk through the gate when Naeem stops me again. "What do you want, kid?" I say exasperatedly. "Is Linolia's opera house as beautiful in person as it is in the pictures?" "More so." I watch him toss the book into the water and slowly shuffle away.
